Everyday Questions

These are the questions people ask at school, in friendships, in families, and in the community.

What does "neurodivergent" mean?

A broad term for a brain that works differently from what society considers typical — differences in attention, learning, communication, sensory processing, movement, or emotional regulation. Not a judgment about intelligence, kindness, or potential.

Is "neurodivergent" a medical diagnosis?

No, it is an umbrella term, not a diagnosis. Never use it to diagnose a classmate from their behavior.

Are all neurodivergent people alike?

No. Two people with the same diagnosis can have different strengths, needs, and communication styles. Get to know the person, not the label.

Are neurodivergent people all geniuses?

No. The "hidden genius" stereotype still measures a person's worth by exceptional performance. Everyone deserves respect without proving a special talent.

Do neurodivergent kids feel less emotion?

No. A person may feel emotions deeply but express them differently or need more time. Different eye contact or tone does not mean they don't care.

How should I talk to a neurodivergent kid?

With the same respect as any peer. Be clear, don't talk down, give processing time. Try: "Would you like me to explain that another way?"

Should I insist on eye contact?

No. Some people listen better while looking away or moving. Listen for meaning, not body language.

"Autistic person" or "person with autism"?

Preferences vary — follow the person's own preference when known. When unknown, listen to how they describe themselves or ask respectfully.

What is a meltdown — is it a tantrum?

A meltdown is an intense response to overwhelm, not a performance. Reduce demands and stimulation, use fewer words, protect safety, allow recovery time.

What is stimming?

Repetitive movement or sound (rocking, humming, fidgeting) that helps a person regulate or focus. Don't stop harmless stimming because it looks different.

Is a quiet student being rude?

Not necessarily. Quiet can be thoughtful. Offer choices: speaking later, writing, pointing, or participating beside the group.

Do accommodations give an unfair advantage?

No — they remove barriers. Extra time or a quieter space changes the route, not the goal.

What should I do if I see bullying?

Don't laugh, forward, or pile on. If safe, say "That's not funny." Check in with the targeted person, save evidence if online, and tell a trusted adult.

What should parents tell their own children?

Keep it simple: "People's brains work in different ways. You don't need to diagnose or fix them. Ask, wait, include, and respect no." Give them a phrase: "Want to join us? No pressure."

Is it okay to ask about someone's diagnosis?

A diagnosis is personal. Ask what's relevant instead: "How do you like to communicate?" or "Would a quieter place help?"

What if I say the wrong thing?

Listen, correct yourself, and change next time: "I interrupted you. I'm sorry. I'll wait next time."

What does real inclusion look like?

Being invited, remembered, and able to participate without hiding who you are. Belonging grows through repeated ordinary actions.
